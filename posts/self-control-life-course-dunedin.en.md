# What Should You Build While You Are Young? A 30-Year Study on Self-Control

"Build things while you are young" sounds like a cliché. But build what, exactly? One study followed a whole generation of children from birth to age 32 and found that **self-control** in childhood is related to health, finances, and criminal records three decades later, and that this follows a continuous "gradient": it is not only the worst-off group that is affected. This article uses that study to talk about "abilities," the kind of thing you can accumulate.

> This is a popular-science summary, not life advice or medical or psychological advice. The paper reports statistical associations at the group level and cannot be used to predict or judge any one person.

## How the study was done

The researchers followed 1,037 children born in 1972–1973 in Dunedin, New Zealand, up to age 32, with **96% of them still in the study**, including those who were doing poorly (Moffitt et al., 2011).

- **How was self-control measured?** Between ages 3 and 11, researchers, teachers, parents, and the children themselves rated nine things (for example impulsivity, inattention, lack of persistence, and aggression), which were combined into one score. These are ratings of everyday behavior, not an inhibition task done in a laboratory.
- **What was looked at at age 32?** Physical exams and blood tests (metabolic abnormalities, airflow limitation, gum disease, sexually transmitted infection, and inflammation), clinical interviews about substance dependence, income, saving and money trouble, and court records of crime.
- **How were other explanations ruled out?** Statistically, family social class and IQ were controlled; in addition, a British twin cohort (509 pairs of same-sex fraternal twins) was used to compare brothers and sisters within the same family.

## What was found

Childhood self-control predicted, at age 32:

- **Health:** physical health problems and substance dependence. It did **not predict depression**.
- **Wealth:** lower socioeconomic position and income, weaker saving and money management, more financial difficulties, and a greater chance that one's children were raised by a single parent.
- **Safety:** the chance of a criminal conviction.

The most intuitive way to see it is to split the population into fifths and compare the top and bottom ends of self-control:

| At age 32 | Highest fifth in self-control | Lowest fifth |
|---|---|---|
| Multiple health problems | 11% | 27% |
| Multiple substance dependencies | 3% | 10% |
| Annual income under NZ$20,000 | 10% | 32% |
| Children raised by a single parent (among those with children) | 26% | 58% |
| Criminal conviction on record | 13% | 43% |

It is also a **gradient**: a little more self-control went with a little better outcomes, and it was not only the lowest group that was at a disadvantage. The result held after removing the lowest fifth and after removing children with a diagnosis of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder.

## The "snares": what happened in between

Children with lower self-control were more likely to step into several "snares" in adolescence: starting to smoke before 15, leaving school with no qualifications, and unplanned teenage parenthood. These snares explained part of the long-term difference, for example 63% for substance dependence and 61% for single-parent child rearing, but not all of it; even among those who avoided these snares, childhood self-control still predicted later outcomes.

## Can self-control change?

The study did not include an intervention, but there is a clue: some children moved up in their self-control rank from childhood to early adulthood, and those people had better outcomes at 32 even after accounting for their starting point in childhood. The authors themselves warn that change that happens naturally is not necessarily the same as change brought about by an intervention.

## Where to be careful

- **This is observational research.** The authors say plainly that the design is observational and correlational and cannot prove cause. Self-control is related to family social class (*r* = .25) and to IQ (*r* = .44); although these were controlled statistically, other factors cannot be ruled out.
- **Effects vary in size.** The predictive power of self-control was roughly comparable to that of IQ and social class; after controls, some effects shrank and some were not significant (for example depression).
- **These are group statistics, not destiny.** A person's self-control changes, and individual outcomes vary a great deal.
- **Cohort and place.** The participants were born in 1970s New Zealand, and whether it holds in other settings today needs other studies.
- **Turning this into inspiration for young people is my extension and was not tested directly in the paper.** The paper studied children, and the authors mostly talk about policy.

## What can you build while you are young?

Within what the paper supports, three points stand on firmer ground:

1. **Treat self-control as a skill you can practice.** That includes delaying gratification, controlling impulses, modulating emotional expression, persistence, and planning. The paper shows it is related to long-term outcomes, and there are signs that it changes.
2. **Try to avoid early decisions that "close doors."** The snares in the study (starting to smoke early, not finishing basic schooling, unplanned parenthood) were one of the important routes by which self-control affected long-term outcomes.
3. **Make good choices the default.** In discussing policy, the authors point out that automatic-enrollment schemes (for example automatic payroll savings) may help people with lower self-control most. Applying that to yourself, for example automatic savings transfers, is my reading; the paper did not test individual-level practices.

# A Gradient of Childhood Self-Control and Adult Health, Wealth, and Public Safety: A Guide to Moffitt et al. (2011)

This summary is based on the paper's full text (the PMC full text; details were checked against it; the page has no page numbers, so sections and tables are cited instead); the bibliographic record and DOI were also checked. The supplementary material (SI Appendix) was not read.

## Research question

Policy-makers are considering large-scale programs aimed at self-control, but whether self-control matters for the health, wealth, and public safety of a whole population was unclear. Moffitt et al. (2011) used the Dunedin Multidisciplinary Health and Development Study to test four hypotheses: (1) whether the prediction from childhood self-control holds along the whole gradient; (2) whether people who moved up in self-control rank had better outcomes; (3) whether adolescent mistakes mediate its influence; and (4) whether preschoolers' self-control already predicts adult outcomes (Introduction).

## Design and measures

- **Cohort:** 1,037 children born in 1972–1973 in Dunedin, New Zealand, followed to age 32 with 96% retention.
- **Self-control (ages 3–11):** nine measures (observer ratings of lack of control at ages 3 and 5; parent, teacher, and self-reports of impulsive aggression, hyperactivity, lack of persistence, inattention, and impulsivity at ages 5, 7, 9, and 11), averaged after principal components analysis into one composite score (internal consistency α = .86). Girls had higher mean levels (*t* = 8.39), but the associations with outcomes were similar across sex, so the sexes were combined and sex was controlled.
- **Covariates:** self-control correlated with family socioeconomic status (*r* = .25) and IQ (*r* = .44), so both were controlled in model 2.
- **Outcomes at 32:** a physical health index (metabolic abnormalities including overweight, airflow limitation, periodontal disease, sexually transmitted infection, and C-reactive protein; 43% none, 37% one, 20% two or more); depression and substance dependence from DSM-IV clinical interviews; informant reports; socioeconomic status, income, single-parent child rearing (only the 47% who had children), financial planfulness, and financial struggles; and court convictions (24% convicted by age 32).
- **Sibling comparison:** 509 same-sex dizygotic twin pairs from the British E-Risk twin cohort (2,232 children), rated at age 5 on the same observational scale, with outcomes at age 12; sibling fixed-effects models.

## Results

**Main outcomes (Table 1, model 2, controlling for social class and IQ)**

| Outcome | Coefficient for low self-control | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Physical health index | IRR = 1.111 (1.020–1.209), *p* = .016 | |
| Recurrent depression | OR = 1.099 (0.849–1.352), *p* = .369 | not significant |
| Substance dependence index | IRR = 1.186 (1.038–1.355), *p* = .012 | informant reports agreed |
| Single-parent child rearing | OR = 1.479 (1.147–1.908), *p* = .003 | |
| Financial struggles | β = 0.156, *p* < .001 | |
| Criminal conviction | OR = 1.714 (1.425–2.063), *p* < .001 | |

- **Gradient:** the linear association remained after removing 61 children with an ADHD diagnosis, after removing the lowest quintile, and after removing both the lowest and the highest quintile.
- **Extreme groups:** highest versus lowest fifth, multiple health problems 11% versus 27%, polysubstance dependence 3% versus 10%, annual income under NZ$20,000 10% versus 32%, single-parent child rearing 26% versus 58%, and conviction 13% versus 43%.
- **Rank change:** childhood self-control correlated *r* = .30 with a young-adult personality measure of self-control; those who moved up in rank had better outcomes at 32 even after controlling for their starting point.
- **Adolescent snares:** children with low self-control were more likely to start smoking by 15 (OR = 1.69, 95% CI [1.45, 1.96]), leave school with no qualifications (OR = 2.28 [1.92, 2.72]), and become unplanned teenage parents (OR = 1.79 [1.40, 2.29]). After accounting for the snares, the effect of self-control was attenuated by 32% for health, 63% for substance dependence, 35% for socioeconomic status, 29% for income, 61% for single-parent child rearing, 35% for financial planfulness, 47% for financial struggles, and 42% for crime; the direct effects mostly remained significant.
- **Preschool (ages 3–5):** the observational scale still predicted health, wealth, and convictions at 32, with modest effect sizes.
- **Sibling comparison (age-12 outcomes):** the sibling with poorer self-control at age 5 was more likely to start smoking by 12 (*B* = 0.07, *p* < .03), do worse in school (*B* = −0.13, *p* < .001), and show antisocial behavior (*B* = 0.09, *p* = .007), still significant after controlling for sibling differences in IQ.

## How to read it

The authors conclude that individual differences in self-control are present in early childhood and predict many outcomes across three decades, separable from intelligence, social class, and family environment, which makes it a target for policy; the timing is consistent with a "one-two punch" of intervention in early childhood and adolescence. The following limitations affect interpretation:

1. **Observational, correlational design:** as the authors state. The sibling comparison and statistical controls reduce some confounding, but genetic and unmeasured family and environmental factors cannot be ruled out.
2. **A composite construct:** self-control is an "umbrella" construct covering impulsivity, attention, persistence, and aggression, built from behavior ratings; it is a different level of measurement from laboratory inhibition tasks (for example the stop-signal task).
3. **Effect sizes:** comparable to the predictive power of IQ and social class, but several coefficients shrink with controls; depression was not predicted; the health-index effect in model 2 is an IRR of 1.11.
4. **Evidence for change is natural history:** the authors caution that naturally occurring change is not the same as intervention-induced change; there was no intervention condition.
5. **The mediation by snares is partial:** attenuation ranged from 29% to 63% and direct effects remained; the choice of snares was "not exhaustive."
6. **Cohort and place:** a 1970s New Zealand cohort; the E-Risk cohort was followed only to age 12.
7. **This article's extension:** turning the results into advice for young people (and individual-level practices such as automatic saving) is inference; the original discusses policy and children.
