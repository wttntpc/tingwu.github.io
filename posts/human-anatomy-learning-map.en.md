# Human Anatomy Learning Map: Understanding Movement and Physiology Through Structure

Anatomy is more than memorizing names. It is about building a map of the body that lets you locate structures, explain function, and connect to injuries and physiological measurements. Learn the common language first, then move from tissues, organs, and systems toward clinical and research questions.

## A suggested five-level path

1. **100 Foundations**: anatomical position, directional terms, body planes, tissues, and homeostasis.
2. **200 Systems**: the skeletal, joint, muscular, nervous, cardiovascular, respiratory, and endocrine systems.
3. **300 Function**: connecting structure to movement, sensation, circulation, breathing, and autonomic regulation.
4. **400 Application**: palpation, movement analysis, common injury mechanisms, and the positions of EEG/ECG/PPG electrodes and sensors.
5. **500 Integration**: working backward from a research question to the anatomical level, measurement sites, and possible confounders it requires.

### Suggested order of use

1. First use an anatomical atlas to confirm bones, joints, muscle origins and insertions, innervation, and standard names.
2. Then use the two books to practice observing three-dimensional continuity, and draw the tissue connections the authors propose.
3. Record "anatomical continuity you can see", "possible force transmission", and "clinical effect" separately, and do not equate the three.
4. Finally, return to peer-reviewed studies to check. Existing systematic reviews support the possibility of tissue continuity and force transmission in some fascial chains, but studies in living humans are still limited and methods vary widely, so interpretation should stay cautious.

## The most effective way to review

For each structure, answer four questions: Where is it? What is next to it? What does it do? What happens when it is injured or measured wrongly? Blank-diagram recall, model localization, and movement demonstrations build spatial relationships better than simply rereading.

# Human Anatomy Learning Map: Understanding Movement and Physiology Through Structure

## Organizing principle

This track replaces isolated memorization with "position, relationship, function, variation, application". The first stage unifies terminology and levels; the second stage focuses on the three axes most relevant to research: the musculoskeletal system and kinetic chains, the nervous system and brain–body communication, and the cardiopulmonary system and physiological signals.

| Module | Must-learn content | Linked research question |
|---|---|---|
| Musculoskeletal | Bony landmarks, joints, muscle origins and insertions, innervation | Movement, load, and injury mechanisms |
| Nervous | CNS/PNS, afferent and efferent pathways, the autonomic nervous system, cerebral vasculature | Cognition, emotion, EEG, and HRV |
| Cardiopulmonary | Cardiac conduction, blood flow, alveolar ventilation and gas exchange | VO₂, ECG, PPG, and the exercise response |
| Surface and imaging | Palpation, electrode positions, sections, and image orientation | Device placement and data interpretation |

## Supplementary path: three-dimensional structural networks and fascial continuity

Traditional regional anatomy provides names, boundaries, and spatial relationships that can be identified repeatedly; models of three-dimensional structural networks and fascial chains can help generate hypotheses about movement and force transmission across regions. The two should not replace each other and should be integrated in the following order:

1. **Establish a baseline.** First use a standard anatomical atlas to master tissue layers, attachment points, neurovascular structures, and joint motion.
2. **Read the clinical viewpoint.** Use Dr. 林兩傳's [《身體的立體結構網絡》 (title translated above)](https://www.books.com.tw/products/0010874057) to understand how the author built skin lines, tendon lines, and bone lines from clinical palpation and treatment experience; treat this content as the author's observational model, not as a validated anatomical classification.
3. **Read the fascial-chain model.** Use Myers's [*Anatomy Trains*, French second edition](https://www.elsevier-masson.fr/anatomy-trains-9782294775031.html) to compare the continuous paths proposed for each myofascial meridian and connect them to posture and movement analysis. The publisher's edition information indicates that this edition includes movement applications, a fascial overview, and online video supplements.
4. **Separate three levels of evidence.** Ask separately (a) whether the tissue is anatomically continuous, (b) whether tension can be transmitted in the living body, and (c) whether interventions based on this model really improve clinical outcomes. Support at one level does not automatically prove the other two.
5. **Return to study design.** Record the study population, tissue path, loading method, measurement tools, and effect size. Systematic reviews show evidence of anatomical continuity and force transmission for some chain segments, but heterogeneity among studies, anatomical variation, and the shortage of evidence in living humans still limit general clinical inference.

> **Where the evidence stands:** fascia is real connective tissue with mechanical function; the "myofascial meridians" are a model used to organize continuous paths. They suit teaching, observation, and forming research hypotheses, and should not be used alone as a diagnosis or a guarantee of treatment effect.

## Criteria for checking

You can locate structures on an unlabeled diagram, explain adjacent structures through three-dimensional relationships, infer the presentation of an injury from function, and say which tissue or physiological phenomenon a research measurement is actually sampling. Normal anatomical variation should be recorded as variation, not automatically treated as abnormal.
