# Athletic Training Learning Map: From Risk Identification to Return to Sport

Athletic training is not "treating whatever hurts". It is a continuous process that runs from prevention through on-site judgment, assessment, management, and rehabilitation to a safe return to sport. At every step you need to know the limits of your own scope of practice and when to refer.

## A suggested five-level path

1. **100 Foundations**: anatomy, physiology, tissue healing, injury terminology, and first-aid principles.
2. **200 Mechanisms**: how load, movement, environment, and personal factors create injury risk.
3. **300 Assessment**: history, observation, palpation, range of motion, strength, functional tests, and red flags.
4. **400 Intervention**: acute care, therapeutic exercise, load management, rehabilitation progression, and outcome monitoring.
5. **500 Decision-making**: use shared decision-making to integrate symptoms, function, risk, sport demands, and referral advice, and plan the return to sport.

## A resource for observing cases: the Sports Injury Video Atlas

The [運動傷害影片圖鑑 (Sports Injury Video Atlas)](https://injury.sportsmedicine.tw/), made by Dr. 吳易澄, organizes real competition footage, reactions after injury, interviews, expert commentary, rehabilitation, and return-to-play records, and can be searched by injured body part, sport, injury situation, and video type (吳易澄, n.d.). It fits in this learning map between "200 Mechanisms, 300 Assessment, and 500 Decision-making" as an entry point for observing cases.

When I use it, I first record the following without looking at the commentary:

1. the sport, the contact situation, and the possible mechanism of injury;
2. the athlete's movement and reaction at the time, and whether red flags might be present;
3. which information about on-site management and the decision to leave play can be observed, and which cannot be determined from video;
4. which named source the later diagnosis, rehabilitation, and return-to-play information comes from;
5. after reading the site's summary, correct my original judgment and note the questions that still need checking.

Videos can only be used to practice observation and to form hypotheses. You cannot diagnose an injury from the footage alone, and you cannot apply a professional athlete's return-to-play time directly to the general public. Formal learning should still return to primary studies, clinical guidelines, and professional assessment; when you cite an individual case, link back to that case page and the sources it lists, rather than citing only this site's notes.

## How to review

Work through the whole process with a case: first judge whether there are red flags for a threat to life or limb, then establish differential directions, choose tests, propose a management and referral plan, and finally state the conditions for returning to play. Do not just memorize the names of positive special tests.

> This article is a learning framework. It does not replace medical diagnosis, emergency-care training, or local regulations. Real cases must be handled within the scope of your credential, your organization's procedures, and interprofessional collaboration.

# Athletic Training Learning Map: From Risk Identification to Return to Sport

## Framework of professional competence

It can be organized by the NATA educational competency areas: evidence-based practice, prevention and health promotion, clinical examination and diagnosis, acute care of injury and illness, therapeutic interventions, psychosocial strategies and referral, and healthcare administration and professional responsibility. The notes on this site are also arranged as a time sequence so that readers can follow a case from prevention before exposure all the way to return to sport.

| Stage | Core question | Records to keep |
|---|---|---|
| Prevention | Which exposures and modifiable factors raise risk? | Baseline, training volume, environment, and history |
| Assessment | Are there red flags? Which hypotheses remain possible? | Subjective and objective data and the reasons for tests |
| Intervention | What are the goals, dose, and progression criteria? | Prescription, response, and adverse events |
| Return | Are health, function, and situational risk acceptable? | Tests, shared decisions, and follow-up |

## How video cases fit into the learning process

The [Sports Injury Video Atlas](https://injury.sportsmedicine.tw/) builds a searchable entry point from public footage of real competitions, reactions after injury, interviews, news, expert commentary, and rehabilitation and return-to-play records, organized by injured body part, sport, injury situation, and video type (吳易澄, n.d.). The resource can be used for case-based observation, but it should not be treated as diagnostic ground truth.

We suggest choosing one case at a time and recording it in a fixed table:

| Level of observation | What to record | Limits of inference |
|---|---|---|
| Exposure and mechanism | Speed, direction, contact, joint position, and external force | The camera angle cannot give the full three-dimensional load |
| Immediate response | Pain behavior, weight bearing, stopping of movement, and leaving play | Fracture, concussion, or internal tissue injury cannot be ruled out from appearance |
| Differential hypotheses | Possible structures, severity, and red flags | Express them as several hypotheses; do not write video observations as a confirmed diagnosis |
| Later evidence | Named diagnoses, imaging, surgery, rehabilitation, and return-to-play reports | Distinguish first-hand statements, media reports, and speculative content |
| Return-to-play decision | Timeline, functional demands, level of competition, and uncertainty | An individual case history cannot be extrapolated directly to a general recovery timeline |

Case notes should link to the original case page in the video atlas and go on to verify the named sources it lists. To discuss diagnostic accuracy, treatment effectiveness, or return-to-play criteria, cite clinical guidelines or peer-reviewed studies separately; the video atlas provides cases and patient-education context, not a substitute for research evidence.

## Boundaries of research and practice

Learning notes should include sensitivity and specificity, pre- and post-test probability, the minimal detectable change, and patient-important outcomes, instead of treating any single test as a diagnosis. Less pain does not mean tissue has fully recovered, and passing a functional test does not mean zero risk; return to sport is a risk-management decision, not a single point in time.
